Pay Me on UPI: How Digital Payments Changed Small Business in India

Chapter 1From Cash Box to Phone Screen: What UPI Is and Why Your Customers Use It

It is a busy evening at your counter. A young man picks up a packet of tea and a bar of soap. Instead of reaching for his wallet, he holds up his phone and asks, "Can I pay by UPI?" You pause. You have heard the word many times, but you have never taken money this way yourself. Your cash box is right there, and it feels safer to stick with what you know. That small moment of doubt is completely normal. In this chapter, we will clear it up together, one simple step at a time.

Let us start with the name. UPI stands for Unified Payments Interface. It is a system that lets people send money from one bank account to another, instantly, using a mobile phone. Think of it as a road that connects bank accounts. The customer's money travels from his bank to yours along that road, and no notes or coins change hands at your counter.

You may wonder who is in charge of this road. That is a fair question when it is your hard-earned money. The National Payments Corporation of India, or NPCI, is an umbrella organisation for operating retail payments and settlement systems in India [1]. Retail payments are simply everyday payments, like the ones made at your counter. NPCI is an initiative of the Reserve Bank of India, or RBI, and the Indian Banks' Association, the group that brings India's banks together [1]. You do not need to know how these bodies work day to day. What matters is that UPI comes from the heart of India's banking world, not from a stranger. UPI was first launched as a small trial on 11th April 2016, when the Governor of the RBI started it in Mumbai [2]. So this is an official system, not some unknown experiment.

Now let us follow one payment from the customer's side. First, he opens a UPI app. A UPI app is simply a phone app, from a bank or a payment company, that people use to send and receive money through UPI. Customers can even link more than one bank account inside a single UPI app [3]. Next, he needs to know where to send the money. The easiest way is a QR code, which is a square pattern of black and white dots that a phone camera can scan to find out where the money should go. You show him your QR code, he points his camera at it, and his app knows it is paying you.

If there is no QR code handy, he can type in your UPI ID instead. A UPI ID, sometimes called a VPA or Virtual Payment Address, is a simple address, a bit like an email address. It lets someone pay you without ever knowing your bank account number. That is a nice bit of privacy for you as a shop owner.

Finally, he types in the amount, say forty rupees, and approves the payment with his UPI PIN. A UPI PIN is a secret number that a person types to approve money leaving their account. It should never be shared with anyone, not even a helpful-sounding caller. Think of it like a shop with two locks. UPI uses two checks to keep payments safe: the first is the mobile number linked to the user's bank, and the second is the UPI PIN [3]. So your customer has to approve each payment himself.

What does a customer need to start using UPI? From what we have just seen, the answer is short. He needs a bank account, a mobile number linked to that bank, a phone with a UPI app, and his own UPI PIN. Once he has these, he is ready to pay. That is why you may meet customers who come in carrying only their phone.

And the numbers show just how ordinary this has become. NPCI's monthly table for August-2026 lists a volume of 24,508.96 million, with a value of 29,82,355.95 crore [4]. Here, volume means the number of payments made, and value means the total money those payments carried, counted in crore rupees. That is a great many payments in just one month. UPI's share of India's digital payments is listed as 85 per cent in FY2025-26, where FY is short for financial year, the yearly period used for keeping accounts [5]. UPI also works round the clock, including Sundays and bank holidays [3]. So a late customer on a festival evening can still pay you without any trouble.

For you, this matters in a very practical way. A student buying a pen or a mother picking up milk may simply hold up a phone to pay. Taking UPI means these customers can pay you the way they find easiest. Accepting it is not about chasing something new. It is about meeting your customers where they already are. You now know how a payment travels from a phone to a bank account. Next, we will set up UPI at your own counter.

Takeaway: UPI is an official way for customers to pay straight from their bank account, and learning it is simply learning how many customers already like to pay.

Chapter 2Setting Up Your Counter: Getting Your QR Code, Knowing the Charges and Receiving Your Money

Ravi runs a small tailoring shop near the bus stand. One morning, a customer collecting a stitched blouse asks if she can pay by UPI. Ravi only has his personal app. He looks at the bare wall beside his sewing machine and wonders if a QR code would fit there. But where does he start? If you are asking the same question, let us walk through it together.

The first choice is between a personal UPI account and a business one. A business account, also called a merchant account, is a UPI account set up for a shop. It gives you a business QR code and keeps your sales separate from your personal money. You can usually get one from your own bank or a trusted payment app. Before opening it, they will do KYC, which stands for Know Your Customer. This is a simple identity check using documents like your ID.

Now, the worry most new owners carry quietly: will every sale cost me money? A bank or payment provider can charge a fee called MDR, short for Merchant Discount Rate, for accepting a digital payment. Since January 2020, MDR has been zero for BHIM-UPI payments and RuPay debit cards, after changes to the law to promote digital payments [1]. Small merchants, including street vendors, who receive up to ₹1 lakh a month through UPI QR codes under the category meant for small sellers will continue to pay no MDR on any payment [2].

There is one special case. Some customers pay from a wallet, which is a store of money kept inside an app and loaded in advance, instead of paying straight from their bank account. With wallets and cards, merchants typically pay a fee called the interchange fee to the wallet or card company [3]. According to NPCI, there is no such fee on payments up to ₹2,000 [3]. A 1.1 per cent fee applies only to merchant payments above ₹2,000, and only for certain kinds of businesses [3]. Most everyday sales at a small counter cost far less than that. If a charge ever puzzles you, ask your bank to explain it.

Next comes settlement. Settlement is the moment the money from a customer's payment actually lands in your bank account and is ready for you to use. Ask your bank or provider clearly when this happens for your account. And build one habit from day one: check that the payment has really arrived before you hand over the goods. A screenshot on a customer's phone is not proof. Your own app or your bank message is.

On a busy evening, you cannot keep picking up your phone. This is where a sound box helps. A sound box is a small speaker kept at the counter that announces aloud each payment you receive. Picture Ravi pinning a hem while a voice calls out that eighty rupees has arrived. He nods, the customer leaves, and nobody waits.

You should also know about limits. A transaction limit is the highest amount that can be paid in a single UPI payment. Banks and NPCI also set daily limits, generally ranging from ₹1 lakh to ₹5 lakh depending on the type of payment, as safety checks [2]. For a small shop, they rarely get in the way.

What about a customer without a smartphone? UPI 123PAY is a way for people with basic phones that lack internet apps to make UPI payments. It works through a voice-response phone number, missed calls, app-based features and sound-based payments [4]. The RBI decided to raise the limit for a single UPI 123PAY payment from ₹5,000 to ₹10,000 [5].

And the customer who only wants to pay ten rupees for a pencil? UPI Lite is a feature for quick, small payments that works from a small balance kept in the app, without typing a PIN each time. It was introduced to make low-value payments faster and PIN-less [4]. The RBI decided to raise the UPI Lite balance limit to ₹5,000 and the limit for a single payment to ₹1,000 [5]. So the chai drinker and the grandmother with a basic phone can both pay you.

The government has also tried to encourage digital payments at small shops. One scheme provided an incentive of 0.15 per cent of the transaction value on payments up to ₹2,000 in the small-merchant category [1]. Ask your bank whether any such scheme is running now and how it works. With your business QR code on the wall, a sound box on the counter and a clear idea of the charges, you are ready to open for business.

Takeaway: Get a business QR code from your bank or a trusted payment app, learn when charges can apply, and always check that the money has reached your account before you hand over the goods.

Chapter 3Staying Safe and Growing: Protecting Your Money and Using Your Payment Record

Picture a busy chai stall in the evening. A young man finishes his tea, holds up his phone and shows you a screen with a green tick. "Paid," he says, already turning to leave. But your sound box stayed quiet, and your hands are full of hot glasses. Feeling unsure here is perfectly sensible. Now that your counter is set up, let us talk about keeping your money safe.

Remember the habit you started on day one. A screenshot is only a picture, and anyone can edit a picture. The real proof is the money showing up in your own UPI app, or your sound box announcing it. So smile and say, "One second, let me check." An honest customer will happily wait. Someone trying to fool you often will not.

Keep an eye out for a few other tricks as well. One uses a collect request, which is a message asking you to approve sending money. If you approve one with your PIN, money leaves your account instead of coming in. A trickster may say, "I am sending you money, just accept this," hoping you will not read carefully. It is also wise to glance at your QR code each morning, to make sure no one has stuck a different one over it. Then there is phishing, a trick where someone pretends to be your bank or a payment company to get your PIN, password or other private details.

The golden rule behind most of these tricks is simple. The RBI says that receiving money does not need you to scan a QR code or enter your PIN, so be careful if anyone asks you to [1]. So if anyone asks for your PIN to send you money, stop and say no.

These tricks are real, but there is some good news too. In FY25, reported fraud incidents fell to 12.64 lakh, and the money involved fell to ₹981 crore [2]. Fraud is still a worry, but the numbers have started to come down [2]. You cannot control everyone else, but a few steady habits at your counter will give you confidence.

Sometimes nothing dishonest happens and a payment simply goes wrong. This is called a failed transaction, a payment that did not go through properly. If money left an account but did not reach the right place, it must be returned automatically, which is called a reversal. Every payment also has a transaction reference number, or UTR, a unique number you use to track it or make a complaint. Note it down whenever something looks odd.

If a transfer leaves one account but does not reach the other, it should be reversed by the day after the payment at the latest, with ₹100 per day paid if the delay goes beyond that [3]. If a customer pays your shop and their account is debited but you get no confirmation, it should be reversed within five days after the payment day, again with ₹100 per day beyond that [3]. Knowing this helps you calmly reassure a worried customer.

If a problem is not fixed, first complain to your bank or the payment company concerned [4]. If you get no reply within 30 days, or you are unhappy with the answer, you can go to the RBI Ombudsman [4]. An Ombudsman is an independent official, appointed by the RBI, who looks into complaints that a bank or payment company has not fixed. This service is free and you can complain online [4]. If you are cheated, report it quickly on the National Cybercrime Reporting Portal or the National Cybercrime Helpline Number 1930 [5].

Now for the good part of going digital. When customers pay through your app, you will see each payment listed there. You can spot which days were busy and how your sales are changing. A kirana owner might notice that Sundays need extra stock. Over time, records like these can support your credit history, which is a record of your money dealings that lenders look at before deciding to lend to you. A senior government finance official has said that when lenders make good use of trusted records held by the government, lending can become quicker and reach more people who find it hard to get a loan [6]. For a small shop like yours, the lesson is simple. The more of your business that is clearly written down, the easier it is to show a lender that you are steady.

Some customers may also pay you with a RuPay credit card on UPI. This is an Indian credit card linked to their UPI app, so they can pay you through UPI using borrowed credit. It will show up in your app the same way as other UPI payments, so nothing new is needed from you. Stay calm, guard your PIN and keep your QR code in sight. Each careful day adds to a record that can help you borrow, stock up and grow.

Takeaway: Always check that money has really arrived in your own app, never share your PIN to receive a payment, and let your growing record of digital sales work for you when you need credit.
