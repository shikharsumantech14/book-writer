Pay Me on UPI: How Digital Payments Changed Small Business in India

Chapter 1From Coins and Change to a Scan: How We Got Here

Picture a busy kirana counter just before lunch. A customer buys a packet of biscuits that costs ten rupees and hands you a five hundred rupee note. You open the drawer and find only a few coins. You send a boy next door to ask for change, the customer waits, and the queue grows behind him. If you have ever helped at a shop, you know this small headache well. Now you are the one behind the counter, and it is good to know there is an easier way.

Cash brings other small troubles too. You count notes at night and count them again in the morning. You carry the money to the bank, stand in line, and lose a part of your day. None of this is hard, but it adds up, and it takes time you would rather spend on your customers.

So let us talk about UPI. UPI stands for Unified Payments Interface. It is a system that lets you send money straight from one bank account to another using your phone. There is no cash in between. The customer pays from their account, and the money lands in yours. To do this, people use a UPI app, which is a phone app that connects to your bank account so you can send and receive money. If you can use WhatsApp, you can learn this.

You may wonder who is behind it. UPI was launched on 25th August 2016 by the National Payments Corporation of India, or NPCI, with the Reserve Bank of India, or RBI, keeping watch over it [1]. NPCI is the organisation that built and runs UPI and other retail payment systems. It is an initiative of the RBI together with the Indian Banks' Association [2]. The RBI is India's central bank, which sets the rules for banks and payment systems. So this is not a side project from a stranger. It sits inside the country's own banking system.

The aim was simple. People needed a quick, common way to pay each other, whichever bank they used. A payment made through a phone, a card or a bank instead of with cash is called a digital payment, and UPI became the most familiar kind. At first, the idea was new. In the first year, FY 2016-17, 44 banks were live on UPI, which means they were connected and ready to take part [1].

Then it grew, and quickly. Annual UPI transactions went from 1.78 crore in FY 2016-17 to over 24,162 crore in FY 2025-26 [1]. That is an almost 13,000-fold surge in transaction volume [1]. A tiny trickle of payments has become a flood. You do not need to remember the numbers; they just show that many people pay this way every day.

Why does this matter to you? Because your customers are probably already used to it. Many will pull out their phones before you even ask. Some apps are especially popular. In February, PhonePe held 45.5% of UPI transaction volume, Google Pay 33.2% and Paytm 7.8% [3]. You will likely see these names on customers' screens, and now you will know what they are.

Even the smallest sellers have joined. Under a scheme called PM SVANidhi, street vendors get cashback incentives of up to ₹1,600 for digital transactions [4]. Cashback simply means a little money returned to the seller. Over 55 lakh beneficiaries were onboarded digitally under the scheme, and together they have carried out over 841 crore digital transactions [4]. Think of the person who sells vegetables from a cart. If that seller can take part, so can you at your counter.

It is natural to feel a little unsure about something new. Most shop owners feel the same way at first. But you are not stepping into an experiment. You are joining a system run by an official national body, watched over by the central bank and used by a great many people every day. Next time a customer holds out a big note and you have no change, you will know there is another way to say yes.

Takeaway: UPI is a widely used, officially run system that your customers already know, so accepting it is a normal part of running your shop and not a risky experiment.

Chapter 2Setting Up Your Shop to Get Paid

It is opening morning, and your shelves are full. A customer picks up a packet of biscuits and a bar of soap, smiles and asks, "Do you have a QR code?" You look at the blank wall behind you and feel a small knot in your stomach. Don't worry. Almost everyone starts exactly here, and the answer is simpler than you think.

A QR code is a small square pattern that a customer scans with their phone camera to pay you. That is all it is. Think of it as your shop's address for money. You can use two kinds of QR code. NPCI, the national body that runs UPI, says UPI supports merchant payments through static and dynamic QR codes [1]. A static QR code is one fixed code that customers scan and then type in the amount. A dynamic QR code is made fresh for each bill, with the amount already filled in. For a general store starting out, a fixed code on the wall is a perfectly good beginning.

Now, what do you need to get going? Only three things: a bank account, a phone and a QR code. You need a bank account, because SBI says the receiver must have one and the amount is credited directly to it instantly [2]. You probably have a savings account already, and that can be your starting point.

Next comes the QR code itself, and you can get it from your bank or from a UPI app. If you bank with SBI, for example, the bank says you can approach your nearest branch to get an SBI UPI QR code [2]. Other banks and apps have their own ways, so a short visit or a call to your branch will tell you the steps. Ask the staff to sit with you while you set it up. Nobody minds, and it is their job.

You will hear a few other names along the way, so let us sort them out. A UPI ID, also called a virtual payment address, is like an email address for your bank account. People can pay you with it without knowing your account number. A UPI PIN is a secret number that you enter to send money. It is yours alone, so keep it private, even from a helpful stranger.

You may also wonder about a personal account versus a merchant account. A merchant account is a bank account or app profile set up for a business to receive customer payments. For small vendors there is also a middle path. The Department of Financial Services says the Person-to-Person-Merchant framework is a category created by NPCI to support small vendors receiving payments directly into their own personal bank accounts [3]. So you do not have to feel stuck if you are not ready for a full business setup. And if you already have a QR stand or soundbox, the Department says merchants do not need to replace, re-register, or alter their existing physical QR stands or soundboxes [3].

Then there is the question every shopkeeper asks: what will this cost me? MDR is a fee that a shop may be charged by its bank each time a customer pays by card or other digital means. A customer paying a shop is called a P2M payment. The Press Information Bureau says a nominal MDR of 0.4% will apply only to P2M transactions above ₹2,000 [4]. That means smaller payments carry no MDR. Think of a customer paying for biscuits, soap or tea, which is just an everyday picture and not a figure from the source. You also do not need GST registration, which is the tax sign-up some businesses must complete, for a small merchant to qualify for zero MDR on UPI [3]. You may also see daily limits on how much can be paid. These are safety limits set by banks and NPCI, not charges [4].

Now to the money itself. Settlement is the step where the money from a payment actually lands in your bank account. NPCI describes UPI as an instant payment system that allows you to instantly transfer money between any two parties' bank accounts [1]. You will also be in good company, because the Press Information Bureau, the government's news office, says UPI serves more than 6.5 crore merchants [5].

Last, build a small routine for checking that each payment really arrived. Never rely on the customer's screen alone. Look for your own proof: a soundbox, which is a small speaker at the counter that announces aloud when a payment has arrived in your account, or the notification on your phone. At closing time, open your app's history and match it with your day's sales. It takes only a few minutes, and it will quickly become as natural as counting the cash drawer. Once that habit is in place, you are ready to open your doors.

Takeaway: Getting started takes only a bank account, a phone and a QR code, so set one up this week and place it where every customer can see it.

Chapter 3Staying Safe and Growing with Digital Payments

Picture Ramesh, who runs a tea stall. A customer finishes two cups of tea, holds up his phone and says, "Done, see the screen." The screen shows a green tick. The queue is long, and Ramesh nearly waves him off. Then he checks his own phone, and no payment is there. This trick is called payment screenshot fraud: a customer shows a fake picture of a payment on their phone even though no money was sent. It can sound worrying, but one small habit helps a lot.

That habit is simple. Never trust the customer's screen. Trust only your side: the notification from your bank or UPI app, or the voice of your soundbox. If you are unsure, open your app and look at the credit yourself. It takes a few seconds, and you are not accusing anyone. You are just being a careful shop owner.

Now let us talk about your PIN. You already know your UPI PIN is yours alone. Here is the part that surprises many people: receiving money never needs a PIN. So if someone says, "Just enter your PIN and the money will come," you can calmly say no.

Some tricks arrive as a collect request, which is a message asking you to approve a payment out of your account. If you are expecting money, never approve one. The same advice says to reject unknown collect requests, to verify the beneficiary name before approving payments, and never to scan QR codes sent by strangers [1]. The beneficiary is simply the person or shop receiving the money.

Sometimes things go wrong even when everyone is honest. First, try your UPI app: open the transaction history, select the payment, tap UPI HELP, choose the reason and confirm [2]. If the problem is a fraudulent transaction, NPCI says to connect with your bank [2]. It also helps to know about a reversal, which is when money from a failed or wrongly processed payment is sent back to the person who paid. For a merchant payment where the shop got no confirmation, the reversal is due within T + 5 days, with ₹100 per day of compensation for delay beyond that [3]. Here, T simply means the day of the payment.

If you still get no answer, you can register a complaint with the RBI Integrated Ombudsman Scheme [3]. Think of it as a route for taking your complaint further when the usual steps have not worked.

If you ever fall victim to a real fraud, act quickly. The Ministry of Home Affairs runs a toll-free helpline, 1930, for reporting financial frauds [4]. Victims are advised to dial it at once, because the helpline works with banks and payment intermediaries to try to freeze the funds before they move out of the fraudster's account [1]. Keep that number saved in your phone, next to your supplier's.

Now the good part. Every digital payment leaves a record, and that record is a gift. At the end of the day you can see what came in without counting slips of paper. Come tax time, tidy records make life easier. The presumptive taxation scheme allows certain taxpayers, such as small businesses, to declare income at a prescribed rate of turnover or receipts, without maintaining regular accounts or undergoing audits [5]. Turnover simply means your total sales. GST is the tax shops collect on sales. GST-registered businesses must retain books of accounts and other records for 72 months (6 years) from the due date of furnishing the annual return for the year relating to those records [6].

That history can also help you borrow. SIDBI has launched the GST Sahay app, which offers small value credit to micro enterprises based on their invoices and trade data [7]. The better your sales are written down, the easier it is to show a lender what your shop earns.

Growing starts with small steps. Keep your QR code where customers can see it. Accept both cash and UPI, so nobody leaves empty-handed. Once a week, look at your history and see which days are busy and which items sell fastest. Then stock a little more of what moves. You have come a long way through these chapters, and you know more than you think. Now go and run your shop with confidence.

Takeaway: Trust the bank notification and never your customer's screenshot, and keep your digital record, because it protects you today and helps you grow tomorrow.
